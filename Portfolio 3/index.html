How Safe Is Flying,
Really?

Two charts. One decade of airline accident data. Two very different impressions of aviation safety.

Charts feel objective, but decisions about axis limits, titles, and framing shape how we interpret data. This project uses the same ten annual accident rates from 2016 through 2025 to demonstrate how visual design can lead readers toward different conclusions.

INTENTIONALLY MISLEADING

Chart 1: Safety Improvements Are Unreliable

Frequent rebounds make the accident rate look alarmingly unstable.

Why it creates this impression: The y-axis starts at 1.0 rather than zero. This zoomed scale magnifies the year-to-year fluctuations and makes reversals appear dramatic. Every point is accurate, but the framing encourages readers to focus on volatility instead of overall improvement.

Vertical axis: 1.0–1.7 accidents per million flights.

CONTEXTUALIZED VIEW

Chart 2: Flying Has Become Safer

The longer-term decline is visible without magnifying every fluctuation.

Why it creates this impression: The zero-based axis puts the changes in perspective, making the overall decline easier to interpret relative to the size of the rates. The rate fell by about 18% over the decade, even though the decrease was not steady.

Vertical axis: 0–2.0 accidents per million flights.

How Easy Is It to Mislead with Data?

It is surprisingly easy to make people interpret the same dataset differently without changing a single number. The first chart uses a truncated y-axis to exaggerate relatively small year-to-year changes, while the second chart uses a wider scale that makes those changes look less dramatic. The choice of title matters too: one directs attention to fluctuations, while the other emphasizes the overall trend. Both charts plot identical values, but viewers may walk away with very different impressions if they do not examine the axes carefully.

To avoid being misled, readers should always check axis starting points, units, time periods, and data sources. It is also important to consider what a chart does not show. Here, accident rates describe accidents per million flights, not the likelihood of a passenger dying on a flight. The rate improved by about 18% from 2016 to 2025, but that does not mean every year was safer than the previous one. A responsible interpretation considers the long-term trend, annual fluctuations, and the meaning of the metric together.

Method and Data

Both charts use the exact same ten annual global all-accident rates and the same year order. The only differences are the vertical scale, color, and editorial framing. Source: International Air Transport Association (IATA), 2025 Full Year Accident Update, page 8. The report notes that accident classifications and historical estimates can be revised; the values here are from the same 2025 edition.

Data file: airline_safety.csv · Both figures: global accidents per million flight sectors, 2016–2025.
